# Microsoft AI Style Guide

**MAI Style Guide Version 1.0 | October 2025**

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Who We Are

At Microsoft AI, we are a collective of creators, connectors, and changemakers working to design technology that earns trust, amplifies human potential, and makes life meaningfully better.

### 1.2 Creative Principles

**Human**
Celebrating the natural elements of light, warmth, earth, science, and the human spirit.

**Emergent**
Progressive in ideas and story. We tell stories that challenge and move the world forward.

**Trusted**
As Microsoft, we imbue trust, safety in an established and carefully crafted identity.

### 1.3 Design System

**Balance Precision and Craft with Experimentation**

MAI begins with people—not just those we aim to serve, but those doing the work from within. We believe that when we help others flourish, we flourish as well. We see emergence not as a theory, but as something lived and felt: the quiet insights, the shared momentum, the joy that rises from real connection. Everything we build is meant to reflect that spirit.

The design system, outlined throughout this document balances precision and craft with experimentation. It adapts, creates space, and is flexible enough to meet the moment with warmth and restraint. It's built to support clarity, not impose structure—letting meaning rise naturally through how things come together.

## 2. Logo

### 2.1 Monogram

The MAI monogram is the brand's primary logo. Simple in form yet distinctive in character, its letterforms are rooted in Segoe Sans but reimagined with subtle flared terminals and stroke contrast, bringing a humanist quality that's unique within the category. This blend of familiar structure and nuanced detail balances precision with warmth, reflecting the brand's core duality: technological sophistication paired with a deeply human perspective.

The overall shape, weight, and spacing of the monogram allow it to hold its clarity and presence at smaller sizes—particularly when contained in a circle or square format.

### 2.2 Wordmark

The Microsoft AI wordmark serves as the secondary logo for the MAI brand. This mark should only be used within internal communications, presentations, team swag, and partners inside the company, but is not intended for external-facing use. When in doubt, please contact MAIbrand@microsoft.com in regards to usage.

The wordmark is an extension of the custom letterforms created for the monogram. Based on the structure and proportions of Segoe Sans, yet refined with the subtle humanist flared stems and shift in stroke width. Together, the marks allow for a flexible identity system, expanding or contracting as needed.

### 2.3 Tertiary Lockups

The monogram may be paired next to a text block to create tertiary logo lockups. These are reserved for internal-facing applications such as swag, apparel, and event materials and not for external brand communications. They provide a space for personality and play within the identity system.

The underlying structure is based on a four-line text block that aligns to the baseline of the monogram. This format offers flexibility, allowing the logo to adapt to different contexts while maintaining its core structure.

### 2.4 Logo Usage

Always ensure the MAI logo is clear, legible, and unobstructed in every application. Whether placed over a solid color, pattern, or image, maintain strong contrast and minimize visual distractions. The logo should stand as a distinct, recognizable focal point within the composition.

### 2.5 Incorrect Usage

The MAI logo is a core element of our identity and should always be presented with clarity, consistency, and respect for its design.

**Do not:**
- Pair the monogram or wordmark with the Microsoft icon
- Use dimensional effects like drop shadows or bevels on the MAI logos
- Place the monogram or wordmark over busy backgrounds—always ensure there is proper contrast and the logo is legible
- Use the monogram and wordmark together or create new lockups with the logo variations

## 3. Typography

### 3.1 Primary Typeface

**Bradford** is the primary typeface for MAI. It should be used for most headlines and also works well for longer text setting.

It's a contemporary take on 'Römische Antiqua' (1884). This widely popular design served as a key reference for Pierpont's hugely successful 'Plantin' and for Stanley Morison's enduring 'Times New Roman'.

One of the primary goals for this typeface was to transpose the physical presence and distinctive character of hot metal type into the digital realm.

*Bradford designed by Laurenz Brunner*

**Styles:**
- Light / Light Italic
- Regular / Regular Italic
- Book / Book Italic
- Medium / Medium Italic
- Bold / Bold Italic
- Extra Bold / Extra Bold Italic
- Black / Black Italic

**Character Set:**
```
ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ
abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz
0123456789&@#?!
```

### 3.2 Supporting Typefaces

**Segoe Sans**
Microsoft's custom humanist sans typeface. It balances warmth and precision, offering a neutral tone with just enough personality. It is familiar to the Microsoft landscape—trusted, legible, and distinctly ours.

*Uses:* Sub-headlines, Body Copy, Typographic Accents

**Red Hat Mono**
A monospaced type family originally commissioned by Paula Scher at Pentagram, and designed by Jeremy Mickel at MCKL Type. This monospaced font can be used to add texture and an added sense of utility.

*Uses:* Typographic Accents, Marginalia, Tech-forward Details

### 3.3 Hierarchy

Size, scale, and position are key to how typography communicates. Ensure noticeable differences between type sizes and textures, and maintain a clear relationship between text elements, whether complementary or contrasting, to create an ideal hierarchy that enhances readability and meaning.

**Example Hierarchy:**

| Element | Typeface | Size/Leading |
|---------|----------|--------------|
| Tag | Red Hat Mono Regular | 18/18 |
| Headline | Bradford Regular | 60/60 |
| Eyebrow | Red Hat Mono Regular | 13/15 |
| Body Text | Bradford Regular | 27/36 |

### 3.4 Setting Type

Typography plays a critical role in delivering messages with clarity and impact. Every detail—type size, leading (line spacing), and tracking (letter-spacing)—affects readability and tone.

As a general rule: the larger the type size, the tighter the tracking and the more compact the leading. Minor kerning adjustments between specific characters may also be needed to achieve optimal visual balance.

**Reference Settings:**

| Type Size | Leading | Tracking |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| 21pt | 24pt | 0% |
| 42pt | 42pt | -1% |
| 84pt | 76pt | -2% |
| 140pt | 120pt | -4% |

### 3.5 Tonal Range

Our typographic system is designed to flex—adapting to the wide spectrum of content the MAI brand carries. From precise, academic writing to news-driven layouts, personal stories, and highly expressive moments, the typographic system has the ability to shift tone without losing cohesion. It can be sophisticated and functional, delivering clarity in technical contexts, or expressive and emotive, bringing warmth and character to storytelling.

### 3.6 Fallback Typefaces

In scenarios where the primary brand typefaces are not available, use these system fonts as substitutes. They maintain clarity, compatibility, and share similarities to the tone of the brand's preferred typefaces.

| Primary Typeface | Fallback |
|-----------------|----------|
| Bradford | Times New Roman (Serif) |
| Segoe Sans | Arial (Sans) |

### 4.3 Content Influenced

As with the other elements of the system, flexibility is built in. In some cases, the content itself can guide the color—shaping the palette to match tone, mood, and subject matter. This adaptability allows the work to feel more connected to its context, creating harmony between imagery and design.

The goal isn't strict uniformity; it's clarity. By letting the content inform the color, the system remains cohesive while giving space for each story to resonate more deeply.

### 4.4 Spectrums

Use gradients subtly—ideal for long-scroll experiences or as soft backdrops, such as dappled light. They should feel organic and seamless, with primary colors transitioning only to other primaries (or within the primary palette) and secondaries transitioning only to other secondaries. Avoid visible jumps and never transition between more than two spectrums within the same view.

### 4.5 Color Proportions

Keep compositions primarily monochromatic to maintain cohesion. Primary colors can be used freely in any composition. Secondary colors may also dominate when appropriate, but only from a single spectrum—never mix secondaries from multiple spectrums within the same composition.

### 4.6 Incorrect Usage

While the palette allows for flexibility, avoid these missteps:
- Using colors from multiple spectrums in a single composition
- Applying solid 100% black or 100% white
- Introducing overly vibrant or unnatural hues
- Using too many colors in a single composition
- Too many colors within a gradient

All colors should feel human, natural, and in harmony with the brand's warm, grounded tone.

## 5. Photography

### 5.1 Art Direction

At MAI, we believe intelligence emerges from the interplay of people, environments, and ideas. Our photography reflects this through authentic moments, layered spaces, and subtle hints of potential. Never staged or overly polished.

Grounded in authenticity, curiosity, and possibility, our visual language reveals rather than performs. We aim to inspire the next generation by showing real individuals working at the frontier of innovation, in spaces where progress unfolds naturally.

Using film-inspired techniques—grain, natural light, soft focus, and candid composition—we capture imagery that feels human, lived-in, and in motion. Guided by three lenses (Individual, Environment, and Possibility), our images invite viewers to step inside, imagine, and see where intelligence is quietly taking shape.

### 5.2 Individual

**Real people, real moments**

**Direction:**
- Capture candid, un-staged moments of focus, curiosity, or collaboration rather than posed group shots or forced smiles
- Frame tight crops—faces, hands, subtle expressions—to invite intimacy and authentic human connection

**Style Notes:**
- Use film or film-inspired photography
- Visible grain, soft focus edges, and natural imperfections
- Shoot in natural light—avoid artificial lighting setups
- Use shadows and contrast to enhance the mood

### 5.3 Environment

**Textural, layered, honest spaces**

**Direction:**
- Photograph real workspaces, studios, or environments in use—not pristine, stylized offices
- Look for depth, reflections, glass, light leaks, and natural framing (doorways, windows) to suggest layers and possibility
- Show environments that feel lived-in, adaptable, and quietly intelligent

**Style Notes:**
- Embrace grain, light flare, and slight motion blur for a lived-in, documentary feel
- Avoid wide-angle perfection—let crooked angles, soft focus, or partial obstructions add texture and reality
- Use ambient light and natural color temperatures
- Lean into warm tones or cool shadows as the space allows

### 5.4 Possibility

**In-between moments, emergent details**

**Direction:**
- Capture transitional, reflective, or unexpected moments—a glance, a pause, a spark of motion
- Include abstract shots: blurred motion, textures, hands in action, light play—suggesting ideas forming or systems in motion

**Style Notes:**
- Incorporate macro or detail shots with shallow depth of field to suggest focus and emergence
- Allow negative space in frame to breathe—possibility lies in what's not fully shown
- Use film-like color grading with soft contrast, desaturated neutral tones, and occasional warm or cool shifts

### 5.5 Treating Imagery

When necessary, use the provided Lightroom presets (.xmp profiles) to apply a subtle, film-inspired tone to select imagery. These treatments are designed to soften digital sharpness, unify color temperature, and create a cohesive visual feel that aligns with the brand's human and editorial direction. The goal isn't to stylize for effect, but to gently refine, creating imagery that feels considered, consistent, and emotionally resonant.

### 5.6 Things to Avoid

Avoid imagery that feels overly corporate, performative, or emotionally exaggerated. Steer clear of stock clichés, high-gloss environments, or generic "tech" tropes. Our photography should feel real, grounded, and human—capturing moments that are honest, not manufactured.

## 6. Illustration

### 6.1 Sketch

The MAI illustration style is based on the idea of sketch. The use of sketch, in its varying forms, is used to capture the process and suggest ideas and dreams. It provides the human touch to the symbiotic relationship with AI.

**Four Forms:**
1. The First Spark
2. Expressive Exploration
3. The Messy Middle
4. Proof in Line

### 6.2 Form 1: The First Spark

This is where an idea begins. Raw, impulsive, and full of possibility. A scribble on a napkin, a scrawl in the margins, or a quick set of unfiltered marks that capture a passing thought. These sketches are not about polish or precision, but about intuition, curiosity, and the courage to start.

This variation on sketch is ideal for moments when conveying the birth of an idea or the thought process of an individual. Use this style for early-stage conceptual work, vision pieces, or to visually represent the seed of an idea before it takes form. When collaborating or working with a specific individual on a thought piece, utilize their unique sketches or visual ideas when possible.

**Key Qualities:**
- Loose, spontaneous linework
- Pencil or pen on neutral backgrounds
- Messy, raw, unresolved

### 6.3 Form 2: Expressive Exploration

This is where observation meets imagination. In a sketchbook-like style that is emotional and human, ideas begin to take shape through textures, repetition, and expressive illustration. These drawings are fluid, may incorporate color, and are often layered with personality, inviting the viewer into the creative process.

Expressive Exploration works beautifully for editorial or thought-leadership pieces, spot illustrations for articles, or anywhere the brand benefits from warmth, character, and visual storytelling that feels handcrafted.

**Key Qualities:**
- Colored pencil, pastel, mixed media
- Hand-drawn, illustrative
- Playful, fluid, and emotionally rich

### 6.4 Form 3: The Messy Middle

Loose lines meet structure. Ideas starting to take form, but they are still imperfect, still personal, and still in-progress. Often bridging analog and digital techniques, this form captures frameworks, relationship mapping, and the connective tissue between concepts.

This variation is well-suited for showing how complex systems come together—maps of ideas, visual strategy frameworks, or diagrams that reflect the evolving nature of a project. It's a space where order and chaos coexist, revealing the beauty of progress in motion.

**Key Qualities:**
- Diagrammatic sketches, organic linework
- Collage, wireframes, relationship mapping
- Messy meets modular

### 6.5 Form 4: Proof in Line

Clarity through iteration. Clean, resolved drawings that visualize complexity with clarity. They are built on layers of thought, balancing precision with the trace of the human hand.

This variation in sketch is the default for visualizing data, processes, and results—ideal for charts, graphs, technical diagrams, or annotated illustrations that need to convey complex information with confidence and clarity.

**Key Qualities:**
- Clean vector-style or technical linework
- Diagrams, annotations, charts
- Built on deep thought and refinement

## 7. Visual Assets

### 7.1 Dappled Light

Dappled light adds depth, warmth, and a sense of motion to compositions. It brings static layouts to life, creating a subtle interplay of light and shadow that feels organic and human. This effect can be used to soften backgrounds, add visual interest, or create a sense of time and place.

In motion, dappled light introduces a gentle rhythm that draws the viewer in without distracting from the core message. Use it sparingly and purposefully—allowing the light to enhance, not overpower, the content. Utilize the provided MP4 files or create new sequences.

### 7.2 Blur

Blur is a versatile visual tool that can be used in both static compositions and motion design. It can soften elements to help them recede into the background, guide focus toward what matters most, or create a sense of depth and atmosphere.

In motion, blur can reveal and conceal, adding a dynamic rhythm to storytelling. Conceptually, blur reflects the balance of clarity and curiosity—the idea that discovery often comes from shifting focus, exploring the unknown, and allowing new details to emerge over time.

Use it to create intrigue, to transition between states, or to subtly direct the viewer's eye without distraction.

### 7.3 Corner Radius

Corner radius is used to soften imagery while preserving its tone and intent. A radius of around **5px** is typically ideal, with a range of **3–8px** offering flexibility to ease sharp edges while maintaining a clean, sophisticated feel.

Avoid overly large rounding, which can appear playful or informal. For buttons, CTAs, and UI elements, fully rounded or pill shapes may be used where appropriate.

## 8. Motion

### 8.1 Principles

Our motion language is built upon the following three motion principles that add a rich layer to our visual identity. Ranging from subtle to more expressive, these can be used across typography, imagery and transitions.

**Flow with Human Rhythm**
Use smooth, intentional easing curves to mirror the pace of human gestures and breathing.

**Reveal with Purpose**
Small, deliberate actions that uncover depth, turning a simple idea into something richer and more interconnected.

**Depth through Dimension**
Layer scale, depth, and spatial movement to create experiences that feel alive, connected and experimental.

### 8.2 Curves

These variations of a smooth ease-in/ease-out family differ mainly in intensity, balancing responsiveness and elegance while allowing small variations to create a cohesive, living system.

**Recommended Easing Types:**
- sin-in-out
- cubic-in-out
- quart-in-out
- quint-in-out
- expo-in-out

| Curve | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Sin-in-out | A balanced, all-purpose curve that starts and ends smoothly, for an approachable feel |
| Quart | More subtle |
| Expo-in-out | Slow starts and ends, but rapid acceleration in the middle. Ideal for bold, impactful moments |

**Avoid:**
- Linear easing—can feel mechanical and lifeless
- Bounce curves—can feel distracting or decorative

### 8.3 Dimensionality

Dimensionality in motion design emerges from the interplay of scale, depth, and perspective—simple shifts that, together, create a richer, more immersive world. As elements move, layer, and interact, they reveal complexity beyond their individual parts, allowing motion to feel not just animated, but alive and evolving in space.

### 8.4 Type Behaviors

These core type behaviors use a combination of our principles to establish a simple, but flexible approach for our type in motion. They can be used in combination or as single treatments:

- Type moves upward or downward by line with opacity and blur effects
- Type moves left and right by line with opacity and blur effects
- Typewriter effect
- Fade in/out with opacity and blur effects

### 8.5 Everyday Application

Motion should be practical and purposeful. Apply typographic movement and simple transitions to bring clarity, hierarchy, and pace to everyday content.

**Common Applications:**
- Title card
- Logo end slate
- Lower thirds

## 9. Flexibility

### 9.1 The Adaptive Framework

The MAI brand isn't a set of rules, it is a living system. One built to move fluidly between lead and support roles, depending on the story being told. In a landscape shaped by collaboration, co-creation, and evolving technologies, the system needs to be flexible enough to know when to take center stage and when to step aside.

This is especially true in moments of partnership, where our identity must leave room for others to shine. The goal is not to overpower, but to elevate.

As a brand rooted in AI, the edges are intentionally soft. There are no hard boundaries, only principles to stretch, reinterpret, and build upon. The framework exists to ensure cohesion, not uniformity. To encourage innovation, not impose limits.

### 9.2 A Stage for Emerging Ideas

Knowing when to step back is just as important as knowing when to lead. At times, the MAI brand softens into the background—serving as a quiet frame that supports without distracting. This restraint keeps the spotlight on the story being told, creating space for other voices, ideas, and partnerships to shine.

Utilizing our design system to guide the eye and provide structure, ensuring the subject remains the focal point is the ultimate goal. Even when understated, it maintains a distinct presence, offering harmony, clarity, and a sense of place for emerging ideas.

### 9.3 Expression

The idea of "expression" allows the MAI brand to stretch beyond the expected, venturing outside the guardrails of a style guide. Built into our design system is the freedom to experiment, to be messy, and to push past convention. This flexibility allows new ideas and executions to emerge—ones we can't yet anticipate—while still holding a subtle thread of connection to our core identity.

It's the space where bold risks, boundary-pushing visuals, and unexpected forms take shape, ensuring the brand remains alive, adaptive, and ready for what's next.

**When to Use Expression:**
- Sharing breakthrough stories
- Unveiling cutting-edge technology
- Announcing a leap forward in AI
- Exploring ideas that redefine the field

Here we give shape to the future before it's widely understood, ensuring the brand remains alive, adaptive, and ready for what's next.
